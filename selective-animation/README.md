# selective-animation — per-player targeted animation (Structural Module)

An animation only the player you point at can see. A `VRCRaycast` in `HitPlayers` mode rides your hand, and because a player capsule exists only on its own owner's client, the ray registers a hit on the targeted player's machine and on no other — so their copy of your avatar runs the animation while everyone else's does not. Per-player selectivity at **0 synced bits**: the wearer's FX consumes one native `_Hit` bool.

**Provenance:** generalized from VRLabs' MIT-licensed [`Selective-Animation`](https://github.com/VRLabs/Selective-Animation), which established the mechanism. Its rig predates the SDK's own raycast (it pins `com.vrchat.avatars ^3.7.0`) and reconstructs a hit flag out of geometry: two FinalIK `GrounderIK` raycasts, configured identically and offset along a shared axis, whose end transforms coincide only when both strike the same surface, with a contact sender/receiver pair on those ends turning that coincidence into an animator bool. Native `_Hit` reports the same fact directly, so the second ray, both contacts, the two constraints that carried the ends, the scale immunizer sourcing a never-instantiated `World.prefab`, and the Final IK dependency (with its "testing in Unity is not possible" caveat) all drop out — 16 GameObjects, 4 constraints, 4 FinalIK components and 2 contacts become one component on one anchored GameObject. Mounting and aiming are theirs in substance; this expresses the aim as a checkable vector rather than a rotation set by eye.

This is a **structural** Module: the behaviour lives in the prefab's components, not an animator, so the entry ships a prefab and **no `controller.yaml`** — the gate's compile pass skips it, though its prefab-integrity pass still checks the prefab imports with no missing scripts (`CONVENTIONS.md` §The gate). Behavioural correctness rests on the install check below.

## Interface

- **Params:** out only — `SelectiveAnimation_Hit` (bool), `SelectiveAnimation_Ratio` and `SelectiveAnimation_Distance` (float), from the raycast's `Parameter` prefix. Declare the ones you read in your FX as ordinary animator params; the component writes them per-client. **Never declare them synced.** A natively-driven param is re-driven every frame by the local component, so a synced copy is a wasted bit (`runtime.md` §PhysBones states the rule for the same class of param), and in the emulator a synced raycast param reads the wearer's replicated value on a clone rather than that clone's own ray (`docs/verify.md`) — the opposite of what this entry is for.
- **Seam:** none — nothing merges into any controller. Drop the prefab at the avatar root: `Aim` carries an MA `BoneProxy` resolving `RightHand`, so it lands on the hand with nothing to place. Re-anchor it anywhere that suits the gesture you want (a finger bone, a held prop) by retargeting that one proxy or simply reparenting the GameObject; then set the direction (below). Two consumer surfaces: the `_Hit` param, and `HitPoint`, the raycast's result transform, driven in world space every frame — constrain a marker or payload to it for an aim point.
- **Dependencies:** `com.vrchat.avatars` (`VRCRaycast`) and `nadena.dev.modular-avatar` (`BoneProxy`), both already package dependencies. `VRCRaycast` is a recent SDK addition and both frameworks gate their param-prefix rewriting behind a version define (VRCFury's `VRCSDK_HAS_VRCRAYCAST`, MA's `MA_VRCSDK3_AVATARS_3_10_3_OR_NEWER`) — on an older SDK the prefix will not travel through a rename.
- **Required assets:** none. No renderers, no materials, no menu.

## How it works

**Aim is one vector, and it is worth deriving rather than eyeballing.** The ray direction is `raycastDirection` on the component, a vector in the mounting transform's **local** frame (measured: rotating the transform 90° about Y moved the hit to world +X). Bone *roll* does not enter into it — roll is rotation about the bone's own long axis, so a ray pointing along that axis is invariant under it. What does differ between rigs is which local axis runs along the bone: of five vendor rigs sampled, four ran along local **+Y** and one along **−Z**, which is why the shipped `(0, 1, 0)` is a sensible default and not a guarantee. Derive the exact vector from the rig instead of nudging it:

    // in the mounting bone's local frame, pointing at the next bone out
    rc.RaycastDirection = hand.InverseTransformDirection((indexProximal.position - hand.position).normalized);

Any bone further out along the limb works as the second term (a finger if the rig has one — fingers are optional in the humanoid rig — otherwise the hand itself, read from the forearm). On the rig this entry was verified against, the shipped default sat 20.96° off that exact direction: close enough to read as "out of the hand", far enough to be worth the one line. With arms at rest the ray points at the floor, so this is a gimmick you aim by raising your arm.

**What the ray can hit, and what "selective" therefore means.** `HitPlayers` queries the `Player` and `PlayerLocal` layers (measured: mask `1536`). Each client holds exactly one player collider — its own — so on any given machine the ray can only ever hit that machine's own player, which is precisely why the hit lands on the target and nowhere else (`runtime.md` §Other load-bearing components owns the mechanism and the upstream bug it rests on). Two consequences worth designing around: the wearer's client never registers a hit on the person they are aiming at, and selectivity means *whoever the ray crosses on their own client* — several players standing along the ray are each selected on their own machine, so this targets a line, not a person.

**Whether you can hit yourself turns on where the ray starts.** Your client's only player collider is your own capsule, so it is the only thing a player-mode ray of yours can ever hit — and it does, conditionally. Measured in world mode against a stand-in capsule: an origin **inside** the capsule passes through and reports the next collider beyond (`2.9500` to a wall at 3.0 m with 0.05 m half-thickness), never the capsule it started in, with a raw `Physics.Raycast` control agreeing; an origin **outside** it, aimed across, registers normally (`1.8000` from 2 m out against a 0.2 m radius). A hand mount is the outside case whenever the arm is extended, since reach exceeds any plausible capsule radius — and head-bone displacement, playspace drag or walking through geometry move the rendered body off the capsule regardless of pose. So **self-hits are both available and incidental**: pointing at your own body is a usable way to tag yourself, and pointing at someone *across* your body can tag you when you did not mean to. A consumer that must not self-trigger fences it itself (gate on `IsLocal`, or on a mode the wearer selects). The real capsule's dimensions and where it sits relative to the rendered bones are in-game-only — the solo check below is what settles them for a given rig and pose.

**A result transform is mandatory.** A `VRCRaycast` with `ResultTransform` unset never registers with the raycast command list and never casts — no hit, no params, no error, no diagnostic. `HitPoint` exists for that reason as much as for its own usefulness, and it sits at the prefab root because a result transform must never be the ray's own transform or a parent of it (it would be writing its own frame every frame).

**Miss behaviour and scale, as shipped.** `SnapToEnd` means a miss reads `_Hit` false, `_Distance` at max, `_Ratio` at `1.0`, and puts `HitPoint` at origin + direction × max. The alternative, `DoNothing`, freezes `_Distance`/`_Ratio` at the last hit's values while `_Hit` goes false and leaves `HitPoint` riding its parent, so a consumer reading a distance without gating on `_Hit` reads a stale one; shipping `SnapToEnd` keeps that trap unarmed. `ApplyTransformScale` is off, so reach is absolute metres; turning it on multiplies max distance by the origin transform's Z scale, after which `_Distance` stays absolute world metres while `_Ratio` becomes scale-relative (`runtime.md` §Scale) — that split decides which channel a scale-aware consumer reads. Raycast properties are read **live** each frame, so a runtime write to any of them takes effect (measured by flipping collision mode mid-play), unlike physbone settings which bake at chain init.

| Knob | Where it is authored | What turning it does |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | the raycast's `raycastDirection`, local to `Aim` | where the ray points relative to the mount — derive it per rig as above rather than nudging a rotation |
| Reach | the raycast's `Distance` | how far along that direction a player is still selectable, in absolute metres (`ApplyTransformScale` off) |
| Mount | `Aim`'s `BoneProxy` bone reference, or its parent | which bone carries the ray; re-derive the direction after changing it |
| Hit-point rotation | the raycast's `ApplyRotation` + `AlignmentAxis` | off by default (position only); on, `HitPoint` also orients to the surface, for a decal or a marker that should lie flat |

## Verifying the install

In play, `Aim` must sit **on** its mounting bone; finding it at the avatar-root origin — the wearer's feet — normally means the `BoneProxy` never resolved. One editor-specific caveat before concluding that: in an unfocused or agent-driven editor MA's edit-time placement may simply not have ticked yet, which reads identically. Check the proxy's resolved `target` as well as its position; a resolved target with a stale position is the venue, not the rig.

`_Hit` will read false in the editor no matter what you do, because the editor has no player colliders at all and faking a capsule is a **false pass** — the client strips every collider off a real avatar (`docs/verify.md`). To prove the rig instead, flip `RaycastCollisionMode` to `HitWorlds` in play, put a collider a known distance along the ray, and read `_Distance` and `HitPoint`: measured `0.7253` against a target 1.0 m out with a 0.25 m half-extent, entered off-axis. Flip it back afterwards. This is also the cheapest check that the direction vector is right — a ray pointing out of the back of the hand reads exactly like a correct one until something is in front of it.

Per-player behaviour is in-game only, and it splits into two checks of very different cost. **Solo**, one client, and worth doing first because everything else rests on it: point away from yourself and confirm `_Hit` stays false, then extend your arm and aim the ray back across your own body and confirm it goes true. That pair pins where your capsule actually sits relative to your rendered hand — which decides both whether self-tagging works as a gesture and whether you will trigger it by accident. **Two clients**: selectivity itself, which no single-client venue can show.
